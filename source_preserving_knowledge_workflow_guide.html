Plain-English personal workflow

A source-preserving personal knowledge workflow

This guide describes a way to turn links, transcripts, notes, Google Docs, and Sheets into a useful personal knowledge base while keeping the original material intact. The aim is to make your own ideas easier to find and connect—not to create data for training an LLM.

About the Ilya Sutskever attribution: I could not verify a canonical, publicly documented “Ilya Sutskever workflow” for a personal knowledge base. The process below is a practical source-first workflow, not a claim about his personal method. If you have a specific interview, article, or video in mind, its exact ideas can be compared with this workflow.

The main idea: preserve, interpret, connect

Treat the source and your understanding of it as two different things. Keep the original available and unchanged. Write separate notes that say what the source contains, what you think it means, and how it relates to other ideas. When you later write a guide or report, follow those links back to the original evidence.

Keep three kinds of material separate

1. Original source

The video, transcript, Google Doc, Sheet, webpage, or personal note as it was originally captured. Keep its file or stable link, author, date, and access information.

2. Source note

A short record about one source: what it covers, useful passages, exact quotes or transcript timestamps, and your assessment of its reliability. Link back to the original.

3. Your idea or synthesis note

Your own explanation, question, comparison, or conclusion. Link to the supporting source notes, and mark your interpretation as yours rather than as a direct quote.

4. A finished document

A guide, report, or HTML page made for a particular purpose. It can draw on your synthesis notes, but should still point readers (including your future self) to the underlying sources.

Step-by-step process

  1. Capture without altering. Save a copy when you have permission, or preserve a stable URL to the source. For Google Docs and Sheets, you can keep the original in Drive and link to it from your knowledge base. If you export a copy, label it as an export and record when it was made.
  2. Add a source record. Give it a clear title and add the source type, creator, date, URL or file path, date captured, and a few topics.
  3. Write a brief source note. In your own words, state the subject and why you saved it. Do not try to write a complete summary every time; capture what will help you find and understand it later.
  4. Capture evidence with locations. Save important quotes, claims, or questions alongside page numbers, headings, sheet names, cell ranges, or video timestamps. Keep quotes clearly marked as quotes.
  5. Write a separate idea note when something matters. Explain one idea in your own words and link it to the relevant source note or notes. Add a question or counterexample if it helps you think.
  6. Connect related ideas. Link notes when one supports, challenges, or adds context to another. A link should explain the relationship, not just exist.
  7. Create new documents from the connected notes. Draft a report or HTML page for a specific reader and purpose. Check key statements against the original source before treating the document as final.

A simple folder and note structure

This can be used in Obsidian or any folder-based Markdown setup. Keep the original wherever it is already managed if moving it would cause confusion; a vault can link to Google Drive instead of copying everything.

Example idea note

# Designing for repairability

## Idea
Repairability can extend useful life when replacement parts and repair instructions
are available and the repair is practical for the owner.

## Evidence
- [[Interview about sustainable design]] — 12:30
- [[Product repair study]] — section "Maintenance costs"

## My current view
This seems useful, but repair access and total cost need to be considered too.

## Open question
What evidence compares repair rates across product categories?

Handle each source type carefully

Links and web pages

Save the URL, page title, author or publisher, access date, and a short note. Websites can change or disappear, so keep a permitted archive or excerpt when you need durable access. Respect copyright and site terms.

Video transcripts

Preserve the original video URL and transcript source. Keep timestamps with quotes or important claims so you can return to the exact moment. Correct obvious transcription errors only in a clearly labeled working copy.

Google Docs

Keep the canonical document in Drive and link to it. Record the title and relevant section headings. If you export a snapshot for offline use, note the export date because the live document may later change.

Google Sheets

Keep the original workbook and refer to sheet names, row ranges, or table headings. When capturing data, retain column names and enough context to understand what each value means. Do not detach numbers from units or dates.

Use AI as an assistant, not as the archive

You may use an LLM to draft a source summary or help compare several notes. Keep the original material and your reviewed notes as the durable record. Mark generated text as a draft until you check it. 

Summarize this source for my personal knowledge base.

Return:
- A short description of the source
- Key claims, each with a page, heading, or timestamp
- Useful exact quotes, clearly marked as quotes
- Questions or limitations

Do not add facts that are not in the source.
If you cannot identify a location for a claim, say "Location not identified."

Source:
{{text or selected passage}}

Review before saving: check names, numbers, dates, quotes, and timestamps against the original. AI summaries can miss context or introduce errors. Do not send sensitive material to an external service unless its data handling is acceptable to you.

Turn notes into a new document

  1. Write down the reader and purpose: what should this guide help someone do?
  2. Select the relevant idea notes, not every item in the vault.
  3. Use the notes to make an outline and group related points.
  4. Attach source links or locations to factual claims as you draft.
  5. Check important claims against the original sources and distinguish evidence from opinion.
  6. Save the new document separately and link it back to the notes it used.

A useful test: if you open a sentence in a finished guide months later, can you quickly find the original source and the passage that supports it? If yes, your knowledge base is preserving context rather than just accumulating summaries.

Keep the process lightweight

  • Do not summarize every source in depth; capture what makes it retrievable and useful.
  • Write idea notes only for concepts you expect to revisit or connect.
  • Use a few consistent fields instead of designing a complicated taxonomy too early.
  • Keep the source link with the note so your archive does not become an island.
  • Review and update your own synthesis when new evidence changes your understanding.
In brief: preserve the original, record where it came from, capture evidence with locations, write your interpretation separately, link related ideas, and verify new documents against their sources. This is a personal knowledge workflow, not an LLM training-data process.
